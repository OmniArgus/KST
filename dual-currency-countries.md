# Dual-Currency Countries: USD Alongside a National Currency

Countries where the US dollar and a national currency **both** circulate in everyday life, with an actual exchange rate between them.

*Figures are approximate nominal 2024–25 estimates (IMF / World Bank), rounded. Venezuela, Cuba, Zimbabwe, Afghanistan and Somalia publish unreliable data — treat those rows as indicative only.*

---

## 1. Scope

**Included:** countries with a national currency *and* USD in daily circulation, with a real (floating or moving) exchange rate between them.

**Excluded — fully dollarized, no national currency:**
El Salvador, Marshall Islands, Micronesia, Palau, British Virgin Islands, Turks & Caicos, Bonaire / Sint Eustatius / Saba.
(Euro equivalent: Kosovo, Montenegro.)

**Borderline — coin-only duals at a hard 1:1 peg:**
Panama (balboa coins), Ecuador (centavo coins), Timor-Leste (centavo coins). They keep a national currency, but only as subsidiary coinage with no FX rate, so they behave like a single currency for system-design purposes.

---

## 2. Why USD circulates alongside the national currency

1. **The local currency failed as a store of value.** Hyperinflation or collapse within living memory wiped out savings — Zimbabwe (2008), Venezuela (2016–19), Lebanon (2019–23), Argentina (repeatedly). People don't distrust the currency; they distrust the government that prints it.
2. **No credible central bank.** Deficit monetization, no institutional independence. Using USD imports the Fed's monetary discipline.
3. **A large USD injection event.** UN missions and foreign aid paid in cash dollars: Cambodia (UNTAC 1992–93, ~$1.7B into a tiny economy), Liberia (UNMIL), Iraq, Afghanistan, Timor-Leste. The dollars arrived and never left.
4. **Remittances.** The diaspora sends USD and families spend it directly. Somalia: ~25–35% of GDP.
5. **Exports are USD-priced.** Oil (Iraq, Venezuela), minerals (Zimbabwe, Bolivia), garments (Cambodia), tourism (Cambodia, Lebanon). Exporters hold USD, so the domestic supply chain prices in USD.
6. **Long contracts and big-ticket assets.** Rent, property, cars and school fees are written in USD because no one signs a multi-year deal in a currency that may halve.
7. **Hysteresis.** Once shops, payroll and bank deposits are dollarized, network effects lock it in. Cambodia has had 25 years of peace and growth and is still ~80% dollarized. **Dollarization is a permanent structural feature, not a crisis mode that switches off.**

**Why the national currency survives anyway:** taxes, civil-service payroll, seigniorage revenue, small change below $1, and national sovereignty.

## 4. What the four characteristics show

### FX stability — a stable currency does *not* undo dollarization

Split of the 17 USD countries: roughly **5 stable, 3 moderate, 4 volatile, 4 extreme, with 4 running an official peg the market rejects** (some overlap).

The important finding is that **stability today does not reverse dollarization**. Cambodia's riel has been rock-solid at ~4,000/USD for 25 years and the economy is still ~80% dollarized. Peru's sol is the steadiest currency in Latin America and Peruvians still hold USD savings. Somalia's shilling is stable purely because no one can print it. Once trust is broken, restoring stability does not bring the money back — that is hysteresis (reason 7 above), and it is the single most important fact for anyone designing a system in these markets.

**Currencies that are genuinely safe to hold:** riel, sol, guaraní, dong, Somali shilling — and the rand-zone trio against their anchor.
**Currencies that are not:** ZiG, bolívar, Cuban peso, kip, kyat, Argentine peso, Liberian dollar. Lebanon's lira is stable only because it was repegged *after* losing 98% of its value.

### Official vs parallel rates — the hidden design trap

**Bolivia, Iraq, Myanmar, Cuba** (and Argentina historically) run an official rate that the market ignores, so **more than one exchange rate exists at the same time**: official, bank, parallel/black market, and sometimes a remittance-specific rate. Any system operating there must model exchange rates as a *set* keyed by purpose, not as a single number.

### Friendship with the US — no correlation

Roughly **9 friendly, 6 mixed/neutral, 5 hostile.** Venezuela and Cuba spend dollars daily while denouncing Washington; Liberia and Vietnam use them as partners. Japan and Germany are far closer US allies and use zero dollars domestically.

Dollarization tracks **whether your own money failed**, not who you like. In Cuba's case the hostility *caused* the dollarization: the embargo plus the peso's collapse left the population no alternative.

Alignment also flips with elections — Bolivia moved from anti-US to friendly in a single election in late 2025, Argentina in 2023. Dollarization has never reversed on that timescale.

### Development status — uniformly developing

All are developing economies except Uruguay (high-income, near-developed). Six are least-developed. **No wealthy country dual-circulates a foreign currency**, because wealthy countries have credible central banks.

### Salary — mostly not livable

Only Uruguay, Argentina, Peru, Vietnam and Iraq's public sector support a normal life on local wages. In eleven of these countries the answer is plainly no — households survive on remittances, informal work, or subsistence farming.

That is exactly why dollars matter there: **the dollar is not a convenience, it is the household savings account.**

## 5. Design implications for a dual-currency system

**Core principle: local currency carries transaction volume, USD carries stored value.** Expect many small local-currency payments and few large USD movements. Rounding, minimum balances and fee logic must work correctly at both extremes.

1. **Unit of account vs. medium of exchange.** Prices quoted in USD but settled in local currency, or the reverse. These are separate fields, not one.
2. **The small-change problem.** Cambodia's riel exists mainly because US coins do not circulate. Sub-dollar amounts are always local.
3. **Rate policy.** Daily posted rate vs. live rate; buy/sell spread; who absorbs it on a mixed-tender payment (e.g. paying $10 with a $5 note plus local currency).
4. **Multiple simultaneous rates.** In Bolivia, Iraq, Myanmar and Cuba the official rate is not the real one. Model rates as `(base, quote, rate_type, source, effective_at)` — never a single scalar — and record which rate a transaction actually used.
5. **Redenomination and devaluation events.** Zimbabwe devalued 43% overnight; Venezuela has cut 14 zeros. The ledger must survive a step change and a currency replacement without corrupting historical balances — store the rate used *on* each transaction, never recompute history at today's rate.
6. **Dual-ledger accounting.** Hold balances per currency without auto-conversion, with explicit FX gain/loss recognition.
7. **Regulatory.** Separate reserve requirements, capital controls and reporting per currency; several of these jurisdictions are sanctioned, which constrains correspondent banking.

**Best real-world models to study:** **Cambodia** and **Liberia** — the two where both currencies genuinely coexist at the point of sale, with mixed-tender payments and daily rate posting. Cambodia gives you the stable case, Liberia the volatile one.
